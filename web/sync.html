Sync academy — from the atom to the answer

Five layers, each standing on the one below. Open them in order if GPS is new to you; everything marked live is computed from this station's current data.
L0 Quantum — what a second even is

Since 1967, one second is defined by quantum mechanics: it is exactly 9,192,631,770 cycles of the microwave radiation a caesium-133 atom emits when its lone outer electron flips spin (the “hyperfine transition”). There is no master clock to phone up — the atom is the definition. GPS satellites fly rubidium atomic clocks built on the same principle, so each satellite is a flying realization of the second.

Relativity bends that second: in orbit, weaker gravity (general relativity) makes the sat clock run +45.7 µs/day fast, while its orbital speed (special relativity) makes it run −7.2 µs/day slow — net +38.5 µs/day. GPS therefore builds its sat clocks detuned: 10.22999999543 MHz instead of 10.23 MHz, so they arrive in orbit already compensated. Ignore the effect and ranges would drift by about 10 km per day — positioning would be useless within the hour. Below, a ground clock races an orbit clock at time-lapse speed.

L1 Physics — the wave's journey

Radio waves obey c = f·λ (speed of light = frequency × wavelength): GPS/Galileo L1 at 1575.42 MHz has λ = 19.0 cm, BeiDou B1I at 1561.098 MHz 19.2 cm, the ATSC TV pilot at 602.3 MHz 49.8 cm. The signal leaves the satellite right-hand circularly polarized (RHCP): the wave's electric field spins like a corkscrew, and a matching patch antenna accepts the direct wave while rejecting the opposite-spin first ground bounce — free multipath defense.

The wave arrives at roughly −160 dBW — a tenth of a billionth of a billionth of a watt, about 20 dB below the receiver's own thermal noise. It is recoverable only because of CDMA: every satellite transmits on the same frequency at once, each repeating its own public 1023-chip PRN code; correlating the incoming mush against a local copy of one code pulls that one satellite up out of the noise (the despreading animation below). Satellite motion shifts the received frequency by the Doppler effect — up to ±5 kHz for MEO satellites;

Two atmosphere layers tax the signal differently: the ionosphere delays it 1–15 m and is dispersive (the delay shrinks with frequency², so it can be measured and removed — ); the troposphere adds about 2.3 m at zenith, hits all frequencies equally, and can only be modeled. Multipath — bounces off ground and walls arriving late — is the remaining cm–m wander you can watch live on the phase chart.

L2 Math — from spheres to a fix

Each tracked satellite yields one number: the pseudorange — signal travel time × c, “pseudo” because your receiver's cheap clock adds the same unknown offset to every measurement. Geometrically each pseudorange is a sphere around a satellite; three spheres intersect in at most two points (one usually deep in space). The clock offset is the fourth unknown, so you need a fourth satellite: 4 equations solve x, y, z and time at once.

With more than four satellites the system is over-determined and least squares finds the fix that minimizes the squared residuals; those residuals are the honesty check — how much the measurements disagree with the answer. Satellite geometry multiplies measurement noise into position error by the GDOP factor: spread-out satellites give a tight intersection, clustered ones a sloppy one (animation below; ). Two more workhorses: the correlation peak (sliding the code replica until it lines up — the peak position is the arrival time), and Allan deviation (ADEV), the standard score of clock stability versus averaging time.

L3 Hardware — flying clocks and the radio chain

A GNSS satellite is a flying clock: an atomic frequency standard, a computer that knows its own orbit (and broadcasts the coefficients as the ephemeris), and an L-band transmitter stamping every chip with its clock. On the ground, this station's chain is antenna → MAX2831 RF transceiver → MAX5864 ADC → SGPIO/CPLD → FPGA (CIC decimation, nibble timestamps, TDC) → ARM Cortex-M0/M4 → USB → this page — walk it live in the Inside the HackRF Pro card above.

Every clock in the radio derives from one 10 MHz reference through a Si5351 synthesizer; the discipline loop steers that reference by writing the FPGA clock-correction register, which reprograms the Si5351's fractional dividers. One clock for everything is what makes the whole page coherent: an error in it shifts every band identically, so cross-band agreement isolates and removes it.

L4 Software — how the box listens

Everything after the ADC is software. Acquisition searches a grid of Doppler × code-delay cells until a correlation peak appears (the heatmap below). Then per-channel tracking loops take over: a DLL follows the code's arrival time, a PLL the carrier's phase, an FLL the frequency during pull-in — their live lock durations are in the satellite table. Bit decode reads the 50 bit/s nav message; its ephemeris predicts where the satellite was at transmit time, and its time-of-week anchors absolute time.

The PVT solver turns pseudoranges into position + clock about once a minute; the discipline loop compares the near-stationary GEO carriers against the local clock and nudges the correction register in ±0.1 ppm steps. Architecturally, each producer process writes only its own state file and the server merges them at read time with expiry — no producer can corrupt another, and a dead one simply fades out.
